## 1. Overview  (rewritten)

Modern natural sciences increasingly depend on **foundation models (FMs)** trained at scale: protein language models enable atomic‑level structure prediction [1]; pathology FMs support clinical‑grade analysis across cancers [2]; geospatial FMs provide task‑general representations for Earth observation [3]; and single‑cell FMs model transcriptional programs across tissues [4]. Yet training such FMs stresses both **communication** (due to frequent global synchronization in data‑parallel pipelines) and **sample efficiency** (due to the large number of tokens/images/samples required to reach target generalization) [5,6,16].

We propose to reduce training cost by **designing optimizers that are provably communication‑efficient and sample‑efficient**, and by **automating** the search for such methods with an agentic research system (AiOR). The project focuses on optimization‑theoretic advances—decentralized and compressed updates, local‑update schemes, and curvature‑aware preconditioning—coupled to rigorous evaluation on GPT‑style pre‑training workloads. CaMLSys (Cambridge Machine Learning Systems Laboratory) has prior work at the ML–systems interface, including distributed optimization and communication‑efficient training; here we pursue principled algorithmic improvements that lower synchronization and data demands while preserving convergence guarantees. ([Science][1])

## Objective statement  (rewritten, mathematical)

We view pre‑training as minimizing an expected risk (F(w)=\mathbb{E}*{\xi\sim\mathcal{D}}[f(w;\xi)]) over parameters (w\in\mathbb{R}^d). Our aim is to **discover new optimization algorithms** that, for non‑convex smooth (F), **reduce** (i) the **number of communication rounds** (R), (ii) the **stochastic gradient evaluations** (T), and (iii) the **energy proxy** (E) (e.g., GPU‑hours or joules) **to reach a target validation loss/perplexity**, while maintaining **non‑asymptotic convergence guarantees** (e.g., (\min*{t\le T}\mathbb{E}|\nabla F(w_t)|^2\le \varepsilon)) and **baseline generalization**. Formally, we target algorithms with improved ((R,T,E)) trade‑offs relative to DDP‑SGD/Adam baselines [5,6], and with robustness under practical network conditions (delay, packet loss) typical of multi‑site training. ([VLDB][2])

## 2. The Research Challenge: Discovering Hyper‑Efficient Optimizers  (rewritten)

### Base problem: communication‑efficient distributed optimization

**Setting.** In standard data‑parallel training, each iteration computes local gradients and performs an **all‑reduce** to synchronize model replicas; thus communication cost scales with iteration count, parameter dimension, and topology [5,6]. **Decentralized** and **compressed** methods (local/periodic averaging, gossip/push‑sum, gradient quantization/sign) provably reduce communication while preserving convergence for smooth non‑convex objectives under standard assumptions [7–11].

**Target.** Using AiOR, we will **search over distributed optimizer designs**—including local‑SGD periods, mixing matrices, compression levels, and error‑feedback rules—to **achieve a (100\times) reduction in effective synchronization frequency** relative to DDP for GPT‑style pre‑training (1B–7B parameters) **at matched final validation loss**. We will formalize the objective as minimizing total communicated bits (\mathcal{C}) subject to (\mathcal{L}*{\text{val}}\le \mathcal{L}*{\text{DDP}}+\delta) and (\mathrm{Var}[\text{loss}] \le \tau), reporting **((\mathcal{C},R))** and **time‑to‑(\varepsilon)** curves with confidence intervals. ([VLDB][2])

**Scientific impact via communication efficiency.** Reducing synchronization frequency and enabling **geographically distributed training** alleviates single‑site power bottlenecks by spreading load across multiple data centers [12]; supports **inter‑site/long‑haul training** across WAN links [13]; and enables **resource pooling across institutions (including universities)** via open, peer‑to‑peer collaboration frameworks [14,15]. These properties directly improve **access** to FM training for domain scientists who otherwise cannot secure single‑site capacity. ([Epoch AI][3])

### Extension problem: sample‑ and compute‑efficient optimization

**Setting.** For fixed compute, **compute‑optimal scaling** prescribes trading parameters for data to reduce loss (e.g., Chinchilla laws) [16]; in science domains, annotated data are expensive or scarce, motivating optimizers that **reduce tokens/epochs to target accuracy** and pair well with self‑supervised pre‑training [17].

**Target.** AiOR will explore **preconditioning** (e.g., K‑FAC [19], Shampoo and low‑precision variants [21]), **memory‑efficient adaptive methods** (e.g., Adafactor [20]), and **projection‑free/conditional‑gradient** schemes (Frank–Wolfe) for structured constraints [18], seeking algorithms that **lower sample complexity** (T(\varepsilon)) and FLOPs to reach a given (\varepsilon), with explicit iteration/communication bounds. We will analyze stability under large‑batch regimes and noise, and report label‑efficiency in **biomedical imaging** and **omics** proxies where labeled data are limited [17]. ([NeurIPS Proceedings][4])

**Downstream benefit to the natural sciences.** Improved communication efficiency supports **distributed, multi‑country** training runs and inter‑university collaboration [12–15]; improved sample efficiency reduces reliance on scarce labeled/curated data in biomedical and geoscience settings [17]. Together, these advances make FM training **practically accessible** for scientific labs relying on FMs for discovery [1–4]. ([Epoch AI][3])

## 3. The AiOR System: Capabilities and Automation  (shortened & more rigorous)

**Design.** AiOR is a **multi‑agent research system** that performs *algorithm synthesis → theory checks → empirical evaluation* in closed loop. It represents optimizers in a typed DSL (update rules, mixing/averaging operators, compression and preconditioners), and searches this space with **hybrid program search**: (i) **symbolic/evolutionary recombination** of update components and schedules; (ii) **tree search** guided by convergence‑risk heuristics and empirical rewards; and (iii) a lightweight **AI reviewer** that checks proof sketches (e.g., descent lemmas, error‑feedback invariants) before large‑scale runs. The system draws on precedents in **learned optimizers** [Andrychowicz‑16; 10] and **agentic algorithm discovery** [AlphaTensor; 22], and we implement it in a modular style inspired by recent **agentic research assistants** [23]. ([NeurIPS Papers][5])

**Automation scope.** With minimal human input (research direction and resource approval), AiOR (a) proposes candidate optimizers with associated **rate claims** and failure modes, (b) generates **reproducible experiments** (DDP and decentralized baselines; 1B–7B GPT pre‑training), and (c) reports **statistical summaries** (confidence‑bounded time‑to‑(\varepsilon), bits communicated, energy proxies). This end‑to‑end loop is already operational on pilot tasks; ARIA funding scales execution to frontier models. *(~⅓ of original length.)*
